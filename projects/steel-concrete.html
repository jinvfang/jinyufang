Testing & Materials

Material Properties of Steel & Concrete

Materials Testing EngineerMar 2026

Project overview

Tension and compression tests, from raw load data to material properties.

Laboratory tension tests on mild and high-strength steel coupons and compression tests on low- and high-strength concrete cylinders, reduced to full stress-strain curves.

For CIVL2201 Structural Mechanics at the University of Sydney, steel coupons (≈25 mm × 9.9 mm cross-section, 55 mm gauge length) were pulled to failure in a 300 kN Sintech machine under AS1391-2020, and 35-day-aged concrete cylinders (≈99 mm × 300 mm, sulphur-capped) were crushed in a 2000 kN DARTEC machine under AS1012.9-2014. Load and displacement were logged continuously and converted to stress and strain.

From the curves I determined Young’s modulus, yield stress, ultimate strength, and ductility for all four specimens. Both steels showed a linear-elastic region, a clear yield plateau, and necking before ductile fracture; both concretes behaved non-linearly with no yield point and failed by brittle vertical and diagonal cracking into a cone. The discussion tied these differences back to microstructure: dislocation movement in crystalline steel versus the heterogeneous cement-aggregate matrix of concrete.

Key work & outcomes

  • Mild steel: E = 196 GPa, fy = 399 MPa, fu = 502 MPa, 32.5% strain at failure. High-strength steel: E = 202 GPa, fy = 820 MPa, fu = 923 MPa, 16.5%.
  • Low-strength concrete: E = 19.9 GPa, fu = 24.0 MPa. High-strength concrete: E = 43.5 GPa, fu = 48.0 MPa; both ≈0.2% strain at peak stress.
  • Showed that doubling steel strength halves its ductility without changing stiffness, while doubling concrete strength also doubles its stiffness.
  • Quantified steel as roughly 10× stronger, 5–10× stiffer, and far more ductile than concrete.
