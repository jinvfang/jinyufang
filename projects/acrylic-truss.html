Design & Fabrication

Optimized Acrylic Truss Design

Mechanical Design LeadFeb 2025 — May 2025

Project overview

Modeled in MATLAB, refined for strength, built by hand.

An eight-joint acrylic Warren truss designed to carry roughly twice the required 32 oz load, with every member sized from a custom MATLAB buckling model.

Working in a team of three for EK301 (Engineering Mechanics I) at Boston University, we deliberately prioritized maximum load capacity over load-to-cost ratio, treating the extra margin as a safety factor the way real structures are overbuilt for reliability. The final specification required the roller support to sit 1 inch above the pin support, so we reworked the joint layout, recalculated every member length and angle, and kept the geometry symmetric to preserve clean load paths.

Our MATLAB solver computed axial forces in all 13 members under an increasing load, flagged members in compression, and checked each against the class buckling fit Pcrit = 2390·L−1.811. The load was stepped up until the first compression member reached its buckling threshold. Uncertainty was bounded with nominal, strong, and weak cases using the ±1.35 oz fit error, and the lower bound served as a conservative design benchmark. The truss was then cut from acrylic strips to the nearest hundredth of an inch and assembled with fiber-reinforced tape joints wrapped for compression resistance.

Alongside the design, the team completed a case study of the 1978 Hartford Civic Center roof collapse, concluding that computer models must be validated against physical behavior and recommending a safety factor of 3.0 for any real-world version of the truss.

Key work & outcomes

  • Raised the predicted failure load from 50 oz (preliminary 7-joint design) to 65 oz ± 1.35 oz, over twice the 32 oz requirement.
  • Identified Member 1 (8.77 in, 46.83 oz buckling strength) as the critical member; support reactions of 43.4 oz and 21.6 oz.
  • Improved the load-to-cost ratio from 0.282 to 0.310 oz/$ at a total cost of $209.91.
  • Added redundant zero-force members to spread load and avoid a single-member failure mode.
